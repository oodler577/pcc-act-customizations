CONDUCT · OUR CODE OF VIRTUES

Practice being the person you want to work with.

A virtue is a good habit. We build it by practicing: telling the truth, listening, keeping promises, helping someone learn, and admitting when we are wrong. Nobody arrives perfect. The aim is to become better.

Why virtues instead of a giant rulebook?

Thousands of pages of conduct rules still cannot describe every situation people will meet. We believe people work better together when they practice good judgment and good habits, rather than hunt for a rule or a loophole. A rule can tell us what to do in one case; a virtue helps us decide what is right in a new one.

Our approach starts with individual improvement and self-moderation: pause, examine your own choices, and correct yourself. Start with “What should I do better?” rather than “How can I catch someone else doing wrong?”

Three virtues, in everyday language

1. Practical Wisdom: think before you act.

Understand the problem, consider the people affected, and choose a useful response. Ask a question before assuming the worst. Explain a difficult idea in a way your listener can understand. Make work easier without cutting corners that hurt someone else.

Keep the balance: neither rush blindly nor spend forever planning something that never gets done. Add diligence: follow through on the good choice.

2. Spiritedness: care enough to help.

Bring energy and courage to useful work. Fix a bug, share a discovery, welcome a newcomer, or speak honestly about a problem. Strong opinions can help us learn when we explain them with reasons and listen in return.

Keep the balance: neither shrug at problems nor turn disagreement into a fight. Add patience: direct your energy toward solving the problem, without humiliating the person beside you.

3. Good Order: leave things better for the next person.

Keep promises, give credit, look after shared spaces, and make your work understandable. At a conference, that can mean finishing your talk on time, making room for questions, and helping the next speaker get ready. In software, it can mean clear documentation and a fix someone else can maintain.

Keep the balance: neither neglect responsibilities nor make so much process that useful work becomes impossible. Add humility: accept correction and remember that someone else may have a better idea.

A simple test: good, harmful, or neutral?

Before speaking or acting, ask:

  • Is it wise? Am I being truthful and considering the consequences?
  • Is my energy helping? Am I trying to improve something, or just win?
  • Does it leave things in good order? Am I respecting other people, their work, and our shared time?

A choice that serves these goods is worth practicing. A choice that harms them needs changing. Some choices are simply neutral: preferring a different editor, asking a basic question, or having a different taste is not a fault. Consider the purpose, circumstances, and effects; disagreement alone does not make someone bad.

When you get it wrong

Stop, listen, acknowledge what happened, apologize when needed, and repair what you can. Then try again. Good intentions do not erase harmful effects, and practicing self-moderation does not mean leaving someone else to handle a problem alone. Ask a conference organizer for help when you need it.

The goal is growth, not a claim of perfection. These virtues are tools for improving ourselves and working well together. They are not a scorecard for ranking people or a reason to shame someone.
